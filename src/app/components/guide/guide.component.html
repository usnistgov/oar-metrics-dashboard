Your guide to the dashboard

This dashboard tells the story of how the NIST Public Data Repository is used: how much data people download, which datasets are popular, and how that changes over time. This page walks you through everything on it, in plain language. No prior knowledge needed. Feel free to skip to whatever you're curious about.

What this dashboard is

The Public Data Repository (PDR) is where NIST publishes research data for anyone to use. Every time someone downloads a dataset, that activity is recorded. This dashboard gathers all of those records and turns them into charts and summaries so you can see, at a glance, how the repository is being used.

It's a read-only view. Nothing here changes the data or the repository. Think of it as a live scoreboard for data usage.

Where the numbers come from

Everything you see is pulled live from NIST's official metrics service at data.nist.gov. There's no separate database, and nothing is made up or estimated. It's the real usage record.

  • Always fresh. The dashboard refreshes itself automatically every few minutes. Up in the top bar you'll see “Updated 3:42 PM (2 minutes ago)”, so you always know how current the numbers are.
  • Refresh on demand. Want the very latest right now? Click the refresh refresh button in the top bar (or the round refresh button near the headline numbers). The dashboard reloads everything from scratch.
  • All-time by default. Unless a card says otherwise, the totals cover the entire history on record, every month since tracking began.

The headline numbers (top strip)

The row of tiles across the top gives you the big picture in five numbers. Each tile has a small info_outline you can hover for a reminder of what it means.

Total downloads
Every successful dataset download, added up across the whole history of the repository.
Download volume
The total amount of data downloaded, measured in terabytes (TB), or petabytes (PB) once it passes 1,000 TB. This is about the size of what people took, not the number of downloads.
Most recent download
When the newest download happened, anywhere in the repository. A pulse-check that data is actively being used.
Datasets tracked
How many distinct datasets have usage on record.
Tracked since
The earliest month with data, so you know how far back the history goes.

The little green or red arrow on some tiles (for example ▲ 4%) compares this month with last month. The tiny wavy line is a sparkline: a mini trend of the last several months, just to show the shape of things.

The cards, explained

Below the headline strip is a grid of cards, each answering a different question. You won't necessarily see all of them at once; you choose which appear (see Making it your own). Here's what each one tells you.

Monthly Download Sizes

A bar chart of how much data (in TB) was downloaded each month. Tall bars are heavy-traffic months. Great for spotting seasonal patterns or a big release.

Monthly Number of Downloads

Like the one above, but counting how many downloads happened each month rather than their size. A month can have many small downloads or a few huge ones, and comparing the two charts tells you which.

Unique Users per Month

Roughly how many different people downloaded data each month. A good measure of reach: are more people finding the data over time?

Most Accessed Datasets

A leaderboard of the most-used datasets. Use the Sort by menu in the card header to rank them by Downloads, Unique users, Data volume, or Most recent activity. The actions menu more_vert lets you show more entries or open_in_full expand it into a bigger view.

Latest Downloads

The most recent activity as it happens: the datasets people just grabbed, newest first, with a live clock so you can see how current it is.

Popular Science Domains

Which fields of science the most-downloaded data belongs to (materials, chemistry, physics, and so on). It answers “what kind of data are people actually after?”

Monthly Download Heatmap

A grid coloured by activity, with months across and years down, so busy periods glow and quiet ones stay pale. It makes long-term seasonality easy to see at a glance (for example, a recurring summer spike).

Repository Health

A set of at-a-glance vitals for the whole collection:

  • Datasets tracked: how many datasets have usage records.
  • With 1+ download: the share that have been downloaded at least once.
  • Never downloaded: datasets that haven't been used yet.
  • Median / dataset: the “typical” dataset's download count (half are above, half below).
  • Mean / dataset: the average across all datasets.
  • Mean / active dataset: the average among only the ones that have been downloaded.

When the median is much lower than the mean, it means usage is concentrated in a handful of very popular datasets, a common and normal pattern.

Downloads by Collection

Downloads grouped by collection (see the next section). Click a bar, or a row in the little list beneath the chart, to drill into that collection.

DataCite Metrics

Pick a dataset's DOI to see its official DataCite citation and usage badge. Handy if you care about how data is being cited in the literature, not just downloaded.

Collections

Most datasets stand alone, but some are grouped into a collection: a curated theme that ties related datasets together (for example, Forensics Science Data or Additive Manufacturing). A dataset can belong to a collection while still being its own thing.

The Downloads by Collection card shows how much traffic each collection draws. Open one to see:

  • its total downloads and what share of the whole repository that represents;
  • how many datasets it contains, and how much data has been downloaded from it;
  • a note on concentration: whether usage is spread evenly or driven by one standout dataset;
  • the member datasets themselves, ranked, which you can sort and open just like anywhere else.

Making it your own

The dashboard is yours to arrange. Your choices are remembered in this browser, so it'll look the same next time you visit.

  • Reorder cards. Grab a card by its handle drag_indicator and drag it wherever you like.
  • Pin a card to the top. Open a card's menu more_vert and choose Pin to top to keep your most important card front and centre.
  • Hide what you don't need. That same menu has Hide card visibility_off. To bring cards back, open Settings settings (the cog, bottom-right) and tick the ones you want.
  • Start over. In Settings you'll find Reset layout (restore the default order) and Show all (bring back every card).

Watchlist & favourites

Found a dataset you care about? Keep an eye on it. Wherever you see a dataset listed, in Most Accessed, Latest Downloads, or a collection, there's a star.

  • Star it star to add it to your Watchlist card, which collects everything you're tracking in one place.
  • Pin your top three. Inside the watchlist you can pin push_pin up to three datasets so they float to the top: your personal shortlist.
  • It stays with you. Your watchlist and pins are saved in this browser, so they're still here when you come back.

If your watchlist is empty, the card gently reminds you: tap the star on any dataset to start tracking it.

Digging into a dataset

Click any dataset row and a panel slides in from the right with its full story: total downloads, unique users, data volume, when it was last downloaded, and the science domains it belongs to.

From there you can jump straight to:

  • View on data.nist.gov: the dataset's public landing page;
  • View detailed metrics: its full metrics page on the PDR.

Many charts can also be open_in_full expanded for a closer look, where you can narrow the time range (last 12 months, last 24 months, or all) or focus on a particular month or year.

Dark mode & colour

Open Settings settings (the cog in the bottom-right corner) to switch on Dark mode or pick an accent colour, including a custom one. The whole dashboard, charts and all, follows your choice, and it's remembered for next time.

Reading the numbers honestly

A few things worth keeping in mind so you interpret the dashboard fairly:

  • “Unique users” is a count of visits, not verified people. The same person on two different days, or across several datasets, may be counted more than once. Treat it as a strong signal of reach, not a precise headcount.
  • Totals are cumulative. Most figures are all-time, so they only ever grow. Month-by-month charts are where you'll see rises and dips.
  • Duplicates are cleaned up. A dataset is counted once, even where the raw records list it more than once, so the “datasets tracked” number reflects real, distinct datasets.
  • Some datasets have no downloads yet. That's normal. Newer or niche datasets take time to find an audience, which is exactly what Repository Health shows.
  • Your settings are private to your browser. Layout, watchlist and theme live on your device only. Nothing about your preferences is sent anywhere.

Tips & tricks

  • star Star datasets you follow so they're always one glance away in the Watchlist.
  • push_pin Pin your single most-watched card to the top, and your top three datasets within the watchlist.
  • swap_vert Re-sort Most Accessed by users or volume to see a different kind of “most accessed”.
  • grid_view Hide cards you don't use to keep the view focused. You can always bring them back in Settings.
  • insights Compare the two monthly charts (size vs. count) to tell “a few big downloads” apart from “lots of small ones”.
  • dark_mode Try dark mode for long viewing sessions or presentations on a big screen.
